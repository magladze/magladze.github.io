About

An unusual route to the work.

My degree is in Political Science. For a long time I treated that as a detour — the thing I did before the career that counted.

It wasn't. Political Science is the study of how institutions actually behave: who owns what, where authority really sits, which incentives quietly decide an outcome. That is also the short list of things that determine whether a data platform or an AI system survives contact with a real organization. The modeling can be taught. The rest is where these projects actually fail — and it is the part I had already spent four years studying.

The route in was analytics and software engineering, then data architecture, then the applied AI work I do now: product engineering, cloud consulting, and data architecture for a federal healthcare program. In 2023 I founded IntelliSoft Ventures to take that work directly to clients — custom AI implementations carried from discovery and acceptance testing through pilot and into production, tied to outcomes someone can actually measure. Most recently, document intelligence at scale for an enterprise insurance carrier.

I've also written ten-plus technical titles on the libraries and systems I build with. Teaching something is the fastest way to find out whether you really understand it — the books are where I find out.

    In plain terms

    In plain terms

    AI architecture

    RAG & semantic retrieval

    Nobody's retrieval problem is the vector database. It's that the one paragraph that mattered never made it into the prompt — and the model answered anyway, fluently.

    The spine of this is settled and it isn't ours to claim: hybrid retrieval, dense fused with lexical, then a cross-encoder reranker over the top. If a system is still running pure vector search, that's a known fix and a cheap one.

    The judgment is in everything the playbook doesn't cover. Chunking is a design decision, not a token count — headings, tables and code have to survive it, and a chunk that reads fine to a human can be orphaned to a retriever. Exact terms are where dense-only dies silently: part numbers, error codes, surnames.

    And when an answer is wrong it is nearly always one of two things — the right chunk was never retrieved, or it was retrieved and ignored. Those have completely different fixes, so we measure them separately from day one. We force citation into generation and design the system to abstain when confidence is low, because something that will say I don't know is worth more than something that guesses well.

    What we'd ask about yours: what your corpus actually is, and what it costs you when an answer is wrong. A support deflector and a regulated decision are not the same system.

    AI architecture

    Agent runtimes & tool orchestration

    An agent demo takes an afternoon. The runtime takes the rest of the project — and the runtime is the part nobody demos.

    The established answer is durable execution: treat a run as a checkpointed, resumable workflow rather than a stateless loop, keep control flow deterministic and testable, and let the model act only inside bounded nodes. MCP for tool interop, sandboxes for anything with side effects. That consensus is sound.

    Where it gets genuinely hard is the part with no tutorial. Tool schemas are a context tax — load forty of them and reasoning degrades before the agent has done anything, so tools get disclosed on demand rather than dumped in. A long-running task has to survive a crash, a rate limit and a human pause without re-billing work it already finished.

    Authority lives in the runtime, never in the agent: scoped allowlists, approval gates before anything irreversible, and no path by which a model escalates its own permissions. And the honest call, most of the time, is that one well-instrumented loop beats a multi-agent swarm — coordination overhead and cross-agent context contamination usually cost more than they buy.

    What we'd ask about yours: how long a task runs, what has to survive a crash, which actions can't be taken back — and what your team can actually operate at 3am.

    AI architecture

    Evaluation harnesses for quality, latency and cost

    It feels better is not a release criterion, and it is how most LLM features ship.

    The discipline is well established: an offline harness on a curated golden set gating changes in CI, and online observability watching live traffic — on shared metric definitions, so an offline pass and a production trace mean the same thing. Match the evaluator to the task, and let deterministic checks carry the load wherever output has structure.

    The craft is in refusing the easy version. An LLM judge is a measurement instrument, and an uncalibrated one is worse than no eval because it manufactures confidence — position bias, verbosity bias and self-preference are all live, all measurable, and all controllable by swapping order and anchoring against human labels.

    Averages hide the thing that matters: a variant can win on mean quality while quietly degrading a high-value cohort or pushing p95 latency past budget, so the gate sits on slices and on cost per request, not the headline number. And golden sets rot — the day one stops absorbing new production failures it starts manufacturing false confidence, so it gets an owner and a lifecycle like any other asset.

    What we'd ask about yours: what decision this harness has to make and how often, who is authoritative on what good means, and the latency and cost budget it has to live inside.

    AI architecture

    Guardrails, offline benchmarks, online A/B

    Every guardrail is a tax on every request, forever. So the design question is never what should we catch — it's what you are willing to let through, and who eats it when you're wrong.

    Three activities that only work as one loop, and the field supplies the pieces: guardrails contain the downside, offline benchmarks stop a regression shipping, online experiments prove the change earned production. The palette is standard — PII stripping at ingress, injection detection inline, dialog-policy control, output classification, schema validation — layered so each is independently testable.

    Then the calls start, and no framework makes them for you. Each layer gets a latency budget and a stance: fail open, or fail closed? A safety classifier on a customer-facing surface fails closed; a latency-sensitive enrichment check probably fails open. Backwards is its own outage.

    The judgment nobody writes down is that a guardrail firing too often is a product failure, not a safety win — precision matters, because a control people learn to route around protects nothing. And you cannot guardrail your way out of a system that shouldn't be answering the question at all; stacking classifiers on a bad retrieval design is expensive theater. And online, stochastic outputs punish naive testing: a change that lifts the headline while raising refusal rate or cost per request hasn't won anything.

    What we'd ask about yours: what the worst plausible bad output is on this surface, who bears it — customer, regulator, brand — and what you're prepared to let through.

    Data architecture

    Warehouse & lakehouse design

    We open with a question people don't expect from someone selling architecture: does this need a lakehouse at all?

    A great deal of big-data tooling gets deployed against workloads a single well-tuned node would serve faster and cheaper. Where consolidation is genuinely warranted the spine is settled — one governed copy on an open table format, Iceberg where multi-engine neutrality matters, Delta where the estate is already Spark-centric, medallion as a layout convention.

    Two calls make or break it, and neither is the file format. First: the catalog is what actually locks you in. Access policies do not travel between catalogs, so a split catalog means governance you cannot enforce no matter what the architecture diagram claims — one catalog becomes the control plane and every engine routes through it.

    Second: medallion is not a data model. Treat Bronze/Silver/Gold as modeling and you end up with three copies of unmodeled data and no conformed dimensions anywhere. Layout is a discipline too — reflexive partitioning produces a small-file explosion, and then you pay compute to scan it forever.

    What we'd ask about yours: real volume and growth, concurrency and latency SLAs, freshness — batch, CDC or streaming — and which engines are already in the building.

    Data architecture

    Spark / PySpark ETL at scale

    Spark is easy to run and hard to run well. Jobs that pass in dev crawl in production, the bill climbs faster than the data, and correctness problems surface downstream weeks later.

    The baseline isn't controversial: DataFrame and Spark SQL so Catalyst can do its job, adaptive query execution on, data landed in Delta or Iceberg rather than CSV, explicit schemas instead of inference, vectorized UDFs only where custom logic genuinely earns them.

    What separates a fast pipeline from a folklore pipeline is that performance work is diagnostic before it is prescriptive. Sprinkling .cache() and .repartition() because a blog post said so is how a job gets slower and more expensive at the same time.

    Read the Spark UI and the event log, find where the time actually goes — shuffle, skew, spill, small files, one straggler holding a stage hostage — then fix that specific thing: broadcast the right dimension, let AQE coalesce and de-skew, salt only the keys that need it.

    Cost is a design constraint, not a monthly surprise. And correctness gets gated in the orchestration graph, because a pipeline that is green on infrastructure metrics while emitting wrong numbers is the worst failure mode available.

    What we'd ask about yours: volume and growth curve, batch or streaming, the freshness the business actually needs — and where it hurts today, cost or reliability.

    Data architecture

    SQL and NoSQL modeling

    Pick the database first and you will spend the next two years bending your data into it.

    We start from access patterns and invariants — every read and write, its latency and throughput target, the consistency it genuinely requires, and the natural aggregate boundaries in the domain. That artifact chooses the store, not the other way round.

    Said plainly: most of the time it chooses Postgres. JSONB, pgvector, partitioning and the extension ecosystem absorb a large share of what used to justify a separate NoSQL system, and every additional store is an operational tax paid forever. That isn't conservatism, it's arithmetic on the team that has to run it at 3am.

    When a workload does warrant a non-relational model, it gets designed to that store's grain rather than a relational schema ported across: DynamoDB is query-first single-table design — enumerate the access patterns or don't bother; Cassandra is one table per query with a partition key chosen to bound partition size; document stores get aggregates bounded by domain edges, not unbounded arrays that work beautifully until the day they don't. Schema is versioned code, evolved expand-then-contract so deploys stay reversible.

    What we'd ask about yours: your top access patterns and their volumes, the p99 and consistency each one needs, and what your team can already operate.

    Data architecture

    Master data management

    Master data management is where good intentions go to die in a three-year program. We start from the money instead of the model.

    Before choosing a domain or a tool, trace where inconsistent core entities are actually costing something — duplicate customers suppressing lifetime value and blocking cross-sell, unmatched suppliers hiding spend leverage, party ambiguity failing KYC. Master the one domain that pays for itself, define the golden record to the precision that use case needs, prove it, then widen.

    The field's toolkit is real and we use it: standardization and blocking, probabilistic and ML-assisted matching rather than brittle deterministic rules, survivorship and trust scoring with an audit trail, and the four canonical styles — registry, consolidation, coexistence, transactional hub — sized against the actual integration topology.

    The judgment is in what to refuse. The boil-the-ocean multidomain hub that dies before it ships. Match precision treated as a single number, when a marketing merge and an AML screen are nowhere near the same bar. And the quiet killer: mastering entities beautifully and never writing them back to the operational systems where transactions happen — a golden record nobody consumes is a very expensive spreadsheet.

    What we'd ask about yours: which entity is bleeding the most revenue or risk right now, how many systems define it, and whether the trusted record has to flow back.

    Delivery & MLOps

    Terraform / IaC, cloud-native deployment

    Infrastructure assembled by console clicks can't be reproduced, can't be reviewed, and can't tell you what is actually running versus what someone declared eighteen months ago.

    The practice is mature: versioned modular Terraform — or OpenTofu where license posture matters — remote state with native locking, state isolated per environment, secrets kept out of state entirely, and every change through a pipeline that runs plan, policy checks, a security scan and a cost diff before a human opinion enters the room.

    Two distinctions do most of the work. One: provisioning the platform and delivering the application onto it are different concerns, and blurring them is why release day is frightening — Terraform stands up the durable substrate, application delivery rides its own pipeline on top.

    Two: IaC as ceremony is worse than no IaC. Adopting Terraform and still applying from a laptop with no tests and no policy buys the overhead without the guarantee, and leaves you believing something untrue about your own estate. Drift detection matters precisely because the console is still there and somebody will use it at 2am.

    What we'd ask about yours: what is actually deployed today, where state and secrets live right now, and how a change currently reaches production.

    Delivery & MLOps

    CI/CD for models and prompts

    The most dangerous change in an AI product is the one nobody shipped: a provider updates a hosted model underneath you, and a system that passed every gate last week answers differently. No diff, no PR, no rollback target.

    The delivery discipline is borrowed wholesale from software and it works: prompts in version control, eval-gated CI on anything touching a prompt, model or retrieval config, cheap assertions before expensive ones, and staged rollout — shadow, canary with automated rollback, then ramp.

    The judgment starts with what a version even is. A prompt version alone is close to useless; the revertible unit is the prompt plus the model ID, decoding parameters, tool schemas and the retrieval snapshot, pinned as one addressable bundle. Get that right and a 2am rollback is a config toggle, not an archaeology exercise.

    The part with no playbook is that you are gating a distribution, not a build. Software CI asks whether the test passed; this asks whether a stochastic system got meaningfully worse — a threshold somebody has to choose and defend. Too tight and the pipeline cries wolf until people route around it; too loose and it is decoration. And eval theater is the failure to fear most: a green suite on a stale, aggregate-only golden set is worse than none, because the team has stopped looking.

    What we'd ask about yours: what breaks if a bad output ships, what ground truth exists to seed a golden set, and how you'd find out if a provider changed the model this morning.

    Delivery & MLOps

    Observability & incident playbooks

    Most AI systems in production are observable at exactly the layer where nothing important fails. CPU is fine. Latency is fine. The answers have been quietly wrong for a week.

    The instrumentation story is settled and vendor-neutral: OpenTelemetry's GenAI conventions, so every model call, retrieval, agent hop and tool invocation is a span carrying tokens, cost, latency and outcome. SLIs defined on outcomes rather than only the four golden signals, and drift tests matched to the signal you're actually watching.

    Where it gets real is that logging is not evaluation. Capturing every prompt and response into traces nobody scores buys storage cost and a false sense of control. The loop has to close: an online sampling layer scores live traffic, and whatever it catches becomes a labeled offline test, so the same failure cannot happen twice.

    Runbooks rot the same way — written once, never rehearsed, drifting out of sync with the live system and the org chart, so they fail exactly when they are needed. We rehearse them, and we decide up front what even counts as an incident here, because the model is being a bit worse has no pager semantics until somebody makes that call deliberately.

    What we'd ask about yours: which failure mode genuinely threatens revenue and trust in your system, and who gets paged when it does.

    Delivery & MLOps

    RBAC, secrets, PII minimization

    Access, secrets and personal data are one control plane, not three checklists — in an ML system they fail together. A broad service account, a long-lived key in CI and un-minimized training data are the same breach at three different points.

    The direction of travel is clear: authorization as code, with coarse stable roles in RBAC and everything genuinely per-tenant or per-purpose in a policy engine; zero standing privilege for workloads through workload identity and short-TTL brokered secrets; PII detection and de-identification before ingestion rather than after.

    The discipline is in the leaks nobody diagrams. Minimization is not a one-time step — personal data walks back in through application logs, tracing spans, prompt and response capture, and evaluation datasets, which is precisely where people stop looking.

    Enforcement belongs at the lowest layer that can hold it: if the warehouse can do row and column masking natively, the rule goes there, because an application-layer check only protects the paths that go through the application. And anonymized is a claim, not a label — differential privacy with an unstated epsilon is marketing, not a guarantee.

    What we'd ask about yours: which data classes are genuinely in scope, where identity already lives, and how much PII utility the model actually needs.
